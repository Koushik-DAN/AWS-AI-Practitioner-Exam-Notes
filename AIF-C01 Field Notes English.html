How AWS words the questions

Hunt the constraint

Almost every question has one phrase that decides it: "lowest operational overhead", "without training a model", "private, no internet", "least cost", "explain why the model decided". Underline it before reading the options.

Prefer the managed, narrow service

If a pre-built AI service does the job (Transcribe, Textract, Comprehend), it beats building on SageMaker. If the job is generative, Bedrock beats self-hosting. Foundation-level exam: the "most managed" answer is usually right.

Cost ladder

Cheapest fix first: prompt engineering, then RAG, then fine-tuning, then continued pre-training. Pick the cheapest approach that meets the stated need.

Eliminate by category

Remove options from the wrong domain: a security service offered for a model-quality problem, a training technique offered for an inference-cost problem. Two answers usually drop out fast.

Exam signal

The guide says the exam targets someone who uses but does not necessarily build AI on AWS. You will not be asked to write code, tune hyperparameters, or do maths. You will be asked to pick the right tool or concept for a business scenario.

1.1 Core concepts

Exam signal

AI ≠ ML. The slides use MYCIN (1970s, 500+ hand-written rules to diagnose bacterial infections) as the example of AI that is not machine learning. A system following explicit if-then rules is AI; a system that learns from data is ML.

Which learning type?

  • Labels exist, predict a number → supervised regression
  • Labels exist, predict a class → supervised classification
  • No labels, find groups → clustering
  • No labels, spot outliers → anomaly detection
  • Agent, actions, rewards → reinforcement
  • "Pre-train on huge unlabelled text" → self-supervised

Data

Labelled vs unlabelled

Labelled data has the answer attached (image tagged "cat"). It powers supervised learning. Unlabelled data has no answer and powers unsupervised and self-supervised learning.

Structured vs unstructured

Structured: tabular rows and columns, and time series (daily stock prices). Unstructured: text, images, audio, video.

SplitTypical shareUsed for
Training set60–80%Fit the model
Validation set10–20%Tune hyperparameters, compare variants
Test set10–20%Final, untouched performance check

Feature engineering turns raw data into useful inputs: extraction (age from birth date), selection (keep the predictive columns), transformation (normalise scales), creation (price per square foot). For text, use TF-IDF or word embeddings; for images, CNNs extract edges and textures. "Garbage in, garbage out": data quality is the most critical stage.

Model fit, bias and variance

High bias (underfit)High variance (overfit)
SymptomBad on training data tooGood on training, bad on unseen data
CauseModel too simple, weak features (linear model on non-linear data)Too little data, trained too long, model too complex, learns noise
FixMore complex model, more featuresMore data, early stopping, data augmentation, feature selection, regularisation, ensembling
Exam trap

"You can't add hyperparameters" to fix overfitting; you adjust them (more regularisation, fewer epochs, lower complexity). Answers that say "add more hyperparameters" are wrong.

Inferencing

Inferencing is the model making predictions on new data. Choose by latency, payload and traffic pattern.

Real life

A bank scores every card swipe in milliseconds (real-time), re-scores the whole portfolio overnight (batch), lets analysts upload a 600 MB scanned loan file and get an email when it is read (asynchronous), and runs a rarely used internal "summarise this contract" tool on serverless so it costs nothing while idle.

Inference at the edge

An edge device (Raspberry Pi, factory sensor) has little compute and maybe no internet. A Small Language Model (SLM) on the device gives very low latency, small footprint and offline use. An LLM on a remote server is more powerful but slower and needs connectivity.

1.2 When AI helps, and when it does not

AI/ML adds value when

  • It assists human decision making (triage, recommendations)
  • Volume makes human work unscalable (millions of images)
  • Repetitive work can be automated (document processing)
  • Patterns are too complex for hand-written rules

AI/ML is the wrong tool when

  • The answer is deterministic and computable: a deck holds 5 red, 3 blue, 2 yellow cards; P(blue) = 3/10. Just write code.
  • A specific outcome is required, not a prediction
  • Cost-benefit does not pay back the data, training and operating cost
  • There is no usable data
Exam trap

Even LLMs with reasoning are approximate. When a question gives a formula-solvable problem (probability, payroll arithmetic, a fixed business rule), the best answer is conventional code, not an ML model.

Model performance metrics

Classification: the confusion matrix

Predicted positivePredicted negative
Actual positiveTrue positive (TP)False negative (FN)
Actual negativeFalse positive (FP)True negative (TN)

Formulas

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R)
Accuracy = (TP + TN) / all

When to use which

  • Precision: false positives are costly (good email sent to spam)
  • Recall: false negatives are costly (missed fraud, missed disease)
  • F1: balance, especially imbalanced data
  • Accuracy: only for balanced data (rarely best)

AUC-ROC (0 to 1, higher is better; 0.5 is a coin flip) plots true-positive rate against false-positive rate across thresholds, so you can pick the threshold for your business case.

2.1 How generative AI works

Foundation model (FM)

A very large model pre-trained on a wide variety of (mostly unlabelled) data. It can be adapted to many tasks: text generation, summarisation, information extraction, image generation, chatbots, Q&A. Training can cost tens of millions of dollars, which is why you rent one (Bedrock) instead of building one. Some FMs are open source (Meta Llama, Google BERT); others are commercial (OpenAI, Anthropic).

LLM

An FM for language: transformer-based, billions of parameters, trained on books, articles and websites. Does translation, summarisation, Q&A, content creation. Interaction is by prompt.

Text generation is next-token prediction

The model produces a list of candidate next words with probabilities, then an algorithm samples one. Because sampling is random, the same prompt can give different answers: non-deterministic. It is also why hallucinations happen: the model picks plausible words, not verified facts.

"After the rain, the streets were…"Probability
wet0.40
flooded0.25
slippery0.15
empty0.10
muddy / clean / blocked0.05 / 0.03 / 0.02

You can play with this in the temperature, Top-P and Top-K sampler in Domain 3.

Model families

ModelHow it worksExample
Transformer LLMProcesses the whole sentence at once with self-attention, weighting the importance of words. Faster training, more coherent text.GPT, BERT, Claude, Llama, Titan Text
Diffusion modelTraining adds noise to pictures (forward diffusion). Generation reverses it: start from noise, denoise toward the prompt.Stable Diffusion, Nova Canvas
Multimodal modelAccepts and/or produces more than one modality (text + image + audio → text + video).GPT-4o, Nova Pro, Nova 2 Omni

Tokens, chunking, embeddings, vectors

Token

The unit a model reads and writes. Word-based tokenisation splits on words; subword tokenisation also splits long or rare words. Roughly: one token is about three-quarters of an English word. Everything in GenAI (limits, price, speed) is counted in tokens.

Context window

The number of tokens the model can consider at once (prompt + conversation + retrieved text + output). Bigger window = more information and coherence, but more memory, compute and cost. First factor to check when choosing a model.

Chunking

Splitting documents into smaller pieces before embedding them for RAG. Chunk size, overlap and strategy decide what the retriever can find. See Knowledge Bases in Domain 3.

Embedding / vector

An array of numbers (hundreds to thousands of dimensions) that encodes meaning. Similar meaning = nearby vectors. Created by an embeddings model (e.g. Titan Embeddings) and stored in a vector database to power semantic search and RAG.

The FM lifecycle

Token-based pricing

new objective 2.1.4 · slide detail on Bedrock pricing in 2.3
  • Text models bill input tokens and output tokens separately (output usually costs more). Embedding models bill input tokens only. Image models bill per image.
  • More tokens means higher cost and higher latency. Cost drivers you control: prompt length, retrieved context, max output length, model size.
  • Temperature, Top-P and Top-K do not change price and do not change latency.
  • Levers: concise prompts, cap output length, smaller model, prompt caching, batch inference (up to 50% cheaper), right-size context from RAG.

Context engineering

new objective 2.1.5

Prompt engineering is how you word an instruction. Context engineering is deciding everything that goes into the model's context window on each call, and keeping it small and relevant, because the window is finite, every token costs money, and irrelevant text hurts accuracy.

Piece of contextEngineering decision
System prompt and instructionsStable, concise, cacheable at the start of the prompt
Retrieved knowledge (RAG)Retrieve only the top relevant chunks; right chunk size
Tool definitions and resultsExpose only the tools needed; trim large results
Conversation history and memorySummarise old turns; store long-term facts outside the window
Examples (few-shot)A few high-quality examples, not dozens
Real life

A support agent answers "Where is my order?" It needs the customer profile, the last order and the returns policy, not the full 80-page handbook and 200 old chat turns. Context engineering is choosing those three items and dropping the rest.

Agentic AI, MCP and multi-agent systems

new objective 2.1.6 · not in the course slides

A plain LLM answers once. An agent uses an LLM as its reasoning engine, in a loop, to pursue a goal: it plans, calls tools (APIs, databases, code), observes the result, and continues until the goal is met or it hands off to a human.

Model Context Protocol (MCP)

An open standard for connecting AI applications to tools and data. An agent (MCP client) talks to MCP servers that expose tools, resources and prompts in one common format. Without it, every agent needs a custom integration for every system (N × M). With it, build the connector once; any MCP-capable agent can use it. Think "USB-C for AI tools".

On AWS: AgentCore Gateway turns APIs and Lambda functions into MCP tools; Amazon Quick Actions and Kiro connect to MCP servers.

Agent-to-Agent (A2A)

A protocol for agents to discover and talk to other agents (each publishes an "agent card" describing what it can do). MCP connects an agent to tools; A2A connects an agent to agents. Supported by Strands Agents and AgentCore Runtime.

ConceptOne-line definitionReal example

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

RAG lets an FM use a data source outside its training data, so answers are current, specific to your company and can cite sources, without changing the model. Use it when you need real-time or private data in responses.

Agents

A Bedrock Agent carries out multi-step tasks: it coordinates tasks in the right order, passes information between them, calls company APIs through action groups (defined with an OpenAPI schema or a Lambda function), and can consult a Knowledge Base via RAG. Business uses: order handling, IT operations, claims processing, travel booking.

3.2 Prompt engineering

Prompt engineering = developing, designing and optimising prompts to get better FM output without training anything. It is the cheapest improvement and the first thing to try.

Four building blocks

  • Instructions: the task and how to do it
  • Context: external information to guide the model
  • Input data: the thing to process
  • Output indicator: format and length wanted

Negative prompting

Explicitly say what not to do or include: "Do not include technical terms, in-depth data analysis or speculation." Avoids unwanted content, keeps focus, improves clarity.

Naïve vs enhanced prompt

Naïve: "Summarise what is AWS." Enhanced: "Write a 2–3 sentence summary for a beginner's course (instructions + output indicator). I am teaching beginners (context). Here is the article: … (input data). Do not include technical configuration (negative)."

Exam signal

"Think step by step" = chain-of-thought. "Here are two examples…" = few-shot. No examples = zero-shot.

Best practices

  • Be specific and concise: state task, audience, format and length
  • Give examples and structure; use multiple comments or delimiters to separate parts
  • Experiment and iterate; compare variants
  • Add guardrails; discover what the model can and cannot do
  • Use system prompts to set behaviour

Reinforcement fine-tuning: example

Prompt: "My app is running very slowly." Judge instructions: be empathetic and run diagnostics with the user.

  • "Restart the app" → 5.0 (superficial)
  • "That sounds frustrating… when did it start?" → 9.0
  • "Open a ticket and attach logs A, B, C" → 2.0 (pushes work to user)

The model iterates toward higher-scoring behaviour.

RLHF in four steps

Fine-tuning on Amazon Bedrock

  • Bedrock makes a private copy of the base FM; fine-tuning changes that copy's weights. None of your data trains the base FM.
  • Training data must follow a specific format and be stored in Amazon S3. Not every model can be fine-tuned.
  • Fine-tuning needs experienced ML people: prepare data, tune, evaluate. Re-training a whole FM costs far more than supervised fine-tuning.
  • Running the custom model: on-demand (price per token, for supported imported models) or Provisioned Throughput (committed capacity).
  • Use cases: specific persona or tone, newer information than the base model has, exclusive data (emails, support history), targeted classification.

Preparing data for fine-tuning

Quality

  • Data curation: remove noise and duplicates
  • Correct, consistent labels
  • Representativeness of real use

Size and balance

  • Enough examples for the task (less for supervised than for re-training)
  • Diverse, balanced, inclusive coverage

Governance

  • Know data origin, licence and permission
  • No sensitive data you cannot expose
  • RLHF feedback data from humans

3.4 Evaluating foundation models

Three approaches

Automatic evaluation

Scores computed automatically using built-in or your own prompt datasets and a judge or statistical metrics. Built-in task types: text summarisation, question and answer, text classification, open-ended text generation.

Human-in-the-loop

A work team (your employees or subject-matter experts) rates answers: thumbs up/down, ranking, custom metrics. Best for tone, friendliness, brand fit.

Benchmark datasets

Curated question sets with reference answers that measure accuracy, speed, efficiency, scalability. Some detect bias and discrimination quickly. You can build your own business-specific benchmark.

Automatic metrics

Guidelines for Responsible AI

Small domain, easy marks. Know the eight dimensions, which AWS tool fixes which problem (Guardrails, Clarify, Model Monitor, A2I, Model Cards), and the difference between interpretable and merely explainable.

4.1 Developing AI systems responsibly

Responsible AI means building systems that are transparent and trustworthy and that limit harm, across the whole lifecycle: design, development, deployment, monitoring, evaluation. It is not a one-off review at the end.

The eight core dimensions

Fairness

Real life: a CV-screening model that rejects women at a higher rate than men with the same skills is a fairness failure.

Veracity and robustness

Real life: a medical triage chatbot must stay accurate even with typos, slang or unusual input, and must not invent facts.

Safety

Real life: a mental-health assistant that must never give self-harm instructions. Guardrails with denied topics enforce this.

Controllability

Real life: a human can pause, override or switch off an automated trading agent. Humans stay in charge.

Bias, variance and who gets hurt

Two ways a model fails its users, covered in Domain 1 and examined again here for the effect on demographic groups:

ProblemWhat it isEffect on people
High bias → underfittingModel too simple, misses real patterns. Poor on training and new data.Consistently inaccurate for everyone, or worse for minority groups the simple pattern ignores.
High variance → overfittingMemorises training data. Great on training, poor on new data.Unreliable decisions in the real world; performance varies by group not seen enough in training.
Biased training dataData not representative (sampling bias), labels reflect past prejudice.Model reproduces and scales discrimination (algorithmic bias).
Exam trap

Do not mix the two meanings of "bias". In the bias vs variance trade-off it is a technical error from too-simple assumptions. In responsible AI it is unfair treatment of groups. Read which one the question means.

Dataset characteristics that support responsible AI

Inclusive and diverse

Covers all groups and situations the system will meet: ages, accents, skin tones, languages.

Balanced

No class or group dominates. Fix imbalance with Data Wrangler or by generating more data for under-represented groups.

Curated sources

Known origin, licensed, quality checked, cleaned of offensive or personal content.

Good labels

Label quality checked (Ground Truth, human audits). Wrong labels teach wrong behaviour.

Detecting and monitoring bias and truthfulness

  • Subgroup analysis: compare accuracy and outcomes by group (age, gender, region). A fine overall score can hide a poor subgroup.
  • Analysing label quality and human audits of samples.
  • Continuous monitoring in production for drift (Model Monitor) and human review of low-confidence predictions (A2I).

Generative AI: capabilities and challenges

Capabilities

Adaptability, responsiveness, simplicity, creativity and exploration, data efficiency, personalisation, scalability.

Challenges

Regulatory violations, social risks, data security and privacy, toxicity, hallucinations, interpretability, nondeterminism, plagiarism and cheating.

ChallengeMeaningMitigation
ToxicityOffensive, disturbing or inappropriate output. Hard to define; fine line with censorship (what about quoting a toxic person?).Curate and clean training data; guardrail models and Bedrock Guardrails content filters.
HallucinationPlausible but false statements. Caused by next-word probability sampling.RAG grounding, contextual grounding check, human verification, label output "unverified", educate users.

Legal risks of generative AI

  • Intellectual-property infringement claims: output or training data that copies protected work.
  • Biased model outputs leading to discrimination claims.
  • Loss of customer trust and reputational damage.
  • End-user risk: harm from wrong advice (medical, legal, financial).
  • Hallucinations presented as fact in regulated settings.
Real life

An airline chatbot invents a refund policy and a customer relies on it. The company is held to the bot's statement. Grounding on the real policy (RAG), a contextual-grounding guardrail, and a human hand-off for refunds would have prevented it.

Responsible model selection

Beyond accuracy and cost, choose models with environmental impact and sustainability in mind: smaller models, efficient purpose-built chips, reusing a pre-trained model instead of training from scratch, shared training. AWS Trainium and Inferentia are designed for better performance per watt than general-purpose GPUs for training and inference respectively.

4.2 Transparent and explainable models

Transparency

Being open about what the system is, what data trained it, what it can and cannot do. Documentation: Model Cards, AI Service Cards.

Explainability

Being able to describe a model's behaviour and its inputs/outputs in human terms, even without knowing the exact inner workings. Often enough for regulators.

Interpretability

The degree to which a human can understand the cause of a decision by looking inside the model. Answers "why and how". A stronger demand than explainability.

Black box

Deep neural networks and large ensembles. Strong performance, hard to interpret. Use explainability tools instead.

Interpretability vs performance trade-off

Decision trees: high interpretability

Supervised learning for classification and regression. Splits data into branches by feature values ("is income > $50K?"). Easy to read and draw. Prone to overfitting with too many branches.

Partial Dependence Plots (PDP)

Show how one feature changes the predicted outcome while other features are held constant. Helpful to explain black-box models such as neural networks.

Tools that support transparency and explainability

  • SageMaker Model Cards: standard document for an ML model (data sources, licences, intended use, risk rating, training details, metrics). Supports audits.
  • AWS AI Service Cards: AWS-published responsible-AI documentation for its services (intended use cases, limitations, design choices, best practices).
  • Amazon Bedrock Model Evaluation and SageMaker Clarify: measure accuracy, robustness, toxicity; explain feature contributions.
  • Open-source models, open data and licences: inspectable, but check licence terms before commercial use.

Safety vs transparency

Revealing too much about a model (weights, prompts, training data) helps scrutiny but can help attackers and expose private data. Choose how much to disclose per audience: full detail to auditors, plain-language reasons to end users.

Human-centred design (HCD) for explainable AI

Amplified decision-making

Help people decide well under stress. Clarity, simplicity, usability, reflexivity and accountability.

Unbiased decision-making

Process free of bias. Train decision-makers to recognise and counter their own bias.

Human and AI learning

AI learns from experts (cognitive apprenticeship), personalisation, accessible to a wide range of users.

Practical features: user-feedback mechanisms (thumbs up/down, report a problem) and AI decision transparency (show confidence, sources and a reason, label AI-generated content).

Real life

A bank declines a loan. The app shows "Declined mainly because of: short credit history, high debt-to-income" (Clarify-style feature attributions), offers an appeal button and routes borderline cases to a human reviewer (A2I).

Exam trap

Poisoning attacks the training data (before/while the model is built). Injection, leaking and jailbreaking attack the prompt at inference. A question that says "malicious data in the training set" is poisoning.

Hallucination detection and grounding

  • RAG grounding: answer only from retrieved, trusted documents and cite the source.
  • Contextual grounding check (Guardrails): scores whether the answer is supported by the source and relevant to the question; blocks below a threshold.
  • Output validation: schema checks, fact checks against a database, a second model as judge.
  • Confidence scoring: low confidence → "I don't know" or hand off to a human.
  • Automated Reasoning checks: logic-based verification against a formal policy (high-accuracy rules such as HR or insurance policy).
Beyond the slides

Hallucination detection, confidence scoring, output validation, audit trails and Amazon Bedrock AgentCore are in the v1.1 guide but not in the slides. Know them at definition level.

Audit trail and logging for AI interactions

Record who called which model, with what, and when. Bedrock model invocation logging sends prompts, responses and metadata to CloudWatch Logs and S3. CloudTrail records Bedrock API calls. CloudWatch metrics such as ContentFilteredCount show whether Guardrails are blocking. Retain logs per your policy and protect them from tampering.

Secure data engineering

Assess data quality

Completeness, accuracy, timeliness, consistency; profiling, monitoring and lineage.

Privacy-enhancing technologies

Data masking and obfuscation, encryption, tokenisation, anonymisation.

Data access control

RBAC, fine-grained permissions, SSO and MFA, least privilege, log all access, review regularly.

Data integrity

Complete, consistent, error-free; backups and recovery, audit trails, test the controls.

Real life

A hospital fine-tunes a summariser on clinic notes. Macie scans the S3 bucket for names and ID numbers, Glue/DataBrew tokenises them, the bucket uses SSE-KMS, Bedrock reaches it through a VPC endpoint, and CloudTrail logs every access.

5.2 Governance and compliance

Why governance matters

Builds trust, reduces legal and reputational risk (bias, privacy violations, unintended consequences), and makes AI scalable across the organisation. It sets clear policies and oversight so systems follow law and regulations.

Compliance challenges specific to AI

  • Complexity and opacity: hard to audit how decisions are made.
  • Dynamism: models change as data and retraining change.
  • Emergent capabilities: unintended abilities appear.
  • Unique risks: algorithmic bias, human bias, privacy violations, misinformation.
  • Algorithm accountability: regulations like the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (and US state and city rules) push for transparent, explainable, fair systems.
Regulated workloads

Industries such as financial services, healthcare and aerospace need an extra layer of compliance (regular reporting to agencies, regulated outcomes such as mortgage decisions, audit, archival, special security). If you must meet those frameworks you have a regulated workload. AWS holds 140+ security standards and certifications (ISO, SOC, HIPAA, GDPR, PCI DSS, NIST, ENISA).
